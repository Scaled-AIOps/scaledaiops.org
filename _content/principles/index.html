Principles

Eight principles for deciding how AI is used when the lifecycle, disciplines and pillars do not give a direct answer.

When the model is silent

The lifecycle, disciplines and control pillars cover the common cases. The principles guide decisions where they are silent: a new tool, an unusual change, a request to relax a control. They apply to everyone using AI, from a single engineer choosing how far to trust a suggestion to the board setting risk appetite.

1

Human accountability, always

Skills & Roles

Every AI-assisted outcome has a named human owner who answers for it. AI can draft, propose and execute within limits, but it cannot be accountable to a customer, a regulator or an incident review. Without a named owner, responsibility dissolves between the person who prompted, the person who approved and the tool that generated.

In practice: The engineer who opens a change owns it, whoever or whatever wrote the code • Every agent, prompt and integration in the element register has an owner, and loses its access when the owner leaves • Incident timelines name the human who authorised each AI action.
Anti-pattern: Closing a post-incident review with "the AI generated it", as if that settled who was responsible.
2

Delegate by risk, not by convenience

Guardrails

The risk tier of the work decides how much AI may do, not how easy the tool makes it. Blast radius, reversibility, data sensitivity and regulatory exposure set the tier, and the highest criterion wins. A tool that can refactor a payment service in one step does not make that change low risk.

In practice: Assign the tier at planning, before anything is generated • Let the pipeline's policy gate map the tier to the approvals required: auto-approve, human review or human only • Design agent autonomy levels per tier in Human–AI Workflow Design.
Anti-pattern: Granting an agent write access to production because it was quicker than setting up a scoped, short-lived credential.
3

Provenance by default

Audit Trail

AI involvement is recorded where the work is already recorded: version control, tracker, pipeline and release record. Provenance captures what generated the work, from which context, and who approved it. Recorded at the source, it forms an audit trail that incidents, audits and retirements can query without a separate store.

In practice: Mark AI-assisted changes with a vendor-neutral commit trailer such as AI-assisted: yes • Fail the pipeline provenance check when a change carries no marking • Record AI involvement and the human decision in ADRs.
Anti-pattern: Reconstructing which changes were AI-generated from tool usage logs after an auditor asks.
4

Verify before trust

AI-Augmented Delivery

AI output is a proposal until it has been checked against an agreed intent. Fluent code, confident summaries and plausible configuration can still be wrong, insecure or unlicensed. Verification depth follows the tier, and trust is earned from evidence, not from how convincing the output looks.

In practice: Agree acceptance criteria and tests before generating, then verify against them at the Verify stage • Use AI pre-review for mechanics and keep humans on intent and risk • Run evals on prompts, agents and AI features as part of the pipeline.
Anti-pattern: Approving a large AI-generated pull request because the tests it wrote for itself pass.
5

Context is an asset

Tooling & Context Engineering

Prompts, skills, context packs and tool integrations shape what AI produces as much as the code it touches. Treat them like code: versioned, reviewed, owned and retired. Unmanaged context leaks sensitive data, drifts out of date and makes results impossible to reproduce.

In practice: Keep shared prompts and context packs in version control with review and a named owner • Scan context for secrets and personal data before it reaches a tool • Retire context assets through the Retire stage like any other element; see Tooling & Context Engineering.
Anti-pattern: Team-critical prompts living in personal chat histories, so they vanish when their author changes role.
6

Measure real value

Value & Measurement

Judge AI adoption by outcomes and flow, never by output volume. Lines generated, suggestions accepted and prompts sent say nothing about value and reward the wrong behaviour. Measure teams and systems, not individuals: AI used to evaluate or monitor workers is high-risk under the EU AI Act.

In practice: Baseline cycle time, rework rate, defect escape rate and review burden before a rollout • Compare AI-assisted and other changes on change failure rate • Set cost against measured outcomes in Value & Measurement.
Anti-pattern: A leaderboard of AI suggestions accepted per engineer.
7

Adopt incrementally

Self-Assessment

Introduce AI in small, reversible steps and expand delegation only on evidence. Each step changes an existing practice rather than adding a parallel one, so it can be observed, measured and rolled back. Organisations that skip from ad hoc use to broad autonomy lose the evidence needed to know what worked.

In practice: Start with low-tier work and named pilot teams • Raise delegation limits at the Learn stage, from change failure and rework data • Use the maturity model to pick the next step, not the final state.
Anti-pattern: An organisation-wide mandate to "use AI everywhere" with no baseline, no tiers and no way back.
8

Governed openness

Governance & Guardrails

Prefer open standards and portable assets, inside clear guardrails. Tools and vendors change faster than governance cycles, so controls, provenance and context must survive a change of tool. Openness without guardrails invites shadow AI; guardrails without openness create lock-in.

In practice: Keep an approved tool catalogue with a fast, published path to add new tools • Hold prompts, context and provenance in formats and systems the organisation controls • Assess third-party AI services for exit cost as well as risk in Governance & Guardrails.
Anti-pattern: Provenance and context stored only inside one vendor's platform, so switching tools erases the audit trail.
